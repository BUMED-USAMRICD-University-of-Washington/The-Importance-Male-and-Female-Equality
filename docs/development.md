In anatomy and developmental biology, muscle tissues and skeletal structures do share striking geometric patterns across the body, though the physical development of males and females follows a specific direction along the spine.

The Linear Orientation of the Spine (Embryology)
------------------------------------------------

In both male and female development, the body grows in a strict head-to-tail direction (called the *craniocaudal axis*).

-   The Top End (Cranial): In all humans, regardless of gender, the brain and head always develop at the top end of the spinal cord (the cranial pole of the neural tube).
-   The Bottom End (Caudal): The lower organs, including the reproductive system and the pelvic floor, always develop at the opposite end of the spine (the caudal pole).

The biological differentiation between male and female is driven by genetic switches and hormones acting on the *same* physical orientation, rather than reversing which end of the spine the head forms on.

Sphincter Muscles vs. Nasal Muscles
-----------------------------------

Your observation about the similarities between the sphincter muscles (like the external anal sphincter) and the facial/nasal muscles (like the *orbicularis oris* around the mouth or the *nasalis* around the nose) is structurally very accurate. In anatomy, these are known as annular or circular muscles.

-   The Design: Circular muscles are engineered to act as natural valves. They remain under constant, resting tension to keep a passage closed and relax structurally when a signal is sent to let material or air pass through.
-   The Microscopic Alignment: Under a microscope, both facial and pelvic sphincters are made of concentric rings of muscle fibers. They are highly sensitive, specialized muscle groups that require continuous, precise feedback from the nervous system to maintain control.

Opposing Tension (The Mechanical Concept)
-----------------------------------------

When looking at anatomical forces as being "pulled one way or the other," researchers often look at the pelvic floor mechanics.

During early development, the pelvic bones and muscular walls shape differently to accommodate different functional demands:

-   The Female Matrix: The pelvic floor muscles stretch and expand wider outward to create a wider birth canal, prioritizing flexibility and structural opening.
-   The Male Matrix: The pelvic floor structures pull tighter inward to support a more closed skeletal base, prioritizing narrower, rigid structural anchoring.
